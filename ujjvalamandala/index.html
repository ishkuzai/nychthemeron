उज्ज्वलमण्डल · Ujjvalamaṇḍala

The Ujjvalā circle — a province of Kanakadeśa, and Payasvatī’s first country 𑀉𑀚𑁆𑀚𑁆𑀯𑀮𑀫𑀡𑁆𑀟𑀮  ·  𐨀𐨂𐨗𐨿𐨗𐨿𐨬𐨫𐨨𐨞𐨿𐨜𐨫

Five areas about one city, on the same plan as Bagānrūd: a gentle place to learn in, a poor place with a grievance, a frightening place, and a fourth.

The fourth is where this one departs, and deliberately. Kanakadeśa’s borders are with other Vedan kingdoms and the country is not at war, so there is no march to hold. In its place Payasvatī offers what it has more of than anywhere else in the world: ruins, and the people who were here first. A Pavasian party does not learn soldiering. It learns that the ground it stands on has been somebody else’s three times over.

I. सोनगढ़ Songaṛh — the city

The capital of the golden country, on the उज्ज्वला Ujjvalā, which everybody calls उजला Ujlā.

It has two names and they are not equal. The chancery, the coinage and the court poets say सुवर्णगढ़ Suvarṇagaṛh; every carter, farmer and soldier in the province says सोनगढ़ Songaṛh. The second is the true one — svarṇadurga → suvaṇṇadugga → songaṛh, a plain descent through three tongues — and the first is a re-cutting of the first element into Sanskrit, performed within living memory to make a modern town sound as old as the kingdom’s pretensions require.

Which name a character uses places him instantly, in the eyes of everyone who hears it. It is the cheapest and most reliable social mechanic in the setting, and it costs nothing to run.

What a party finds: the bazaar on the Ujjvalā ghats; the pandits, who are the court’s grammarians, lawyers, astronomers and genealogists at once and regard these as one profession; the Rājā Bhānuvarman, who holds a rite in the old imperial ruin each year to assert an inheritance he cannot document; and Śubhāṅga, his chief pandit, who re-cut the city’s name and would do it again.

II. सुक्षेत्र Sukhet — the good field · Tier I

𑀲𑀼𑀓𑁆𑀱𑁂𑀢𑁆𑀭  ·  𐨯𐨂𐨐𐨿𐨮𐨅𐨟𐨿𐨪

The doab between the Ujjvalā and the मत्स्यवती Matsyavatī, the fish-river — which is a Sanskrit translation of an Ukkul name, as half the water in this province turns out to be.

Rice, cane, mango groves, bunded fields and tanks. It is the richest ground in Kanakadeśa and it pays for the court, and its name means the good field — which nobody believes, because sukh means ease and happiness in the living tongue and this is manifestly the most comfortable country in the kingdom. The pandits know it means field. They say so in footnotes.

Towns: हाथीगाँव Hāthīgā̃v, the elephant village — which appears in the land registers as हत्थिगाम Hatthigāma, and a new party’s first lesson is that these are one place. A good third of the province is like this.

The small troubles are a tiger in the cane, a boundary dispute between two villages that has been running for forty years, a tax-farmer, and an elephant that will not work.

And then the step-well. सातसीढ़ी Sātsīṛhī, the seven stairs: a bāolī going down in seven storeys of carved landings to water that is cold in June. Nobody uses the lowest two. On the seventh landing there is writing, and it is not in any script the village can read, and it is not Brahmi.

It is Ukkul. The first dungeon in the Pavasian starting region contains the oldest secret in the region, carved at eye height, and the party cannot read a word of it. The only people who can live in hill villages eight days east.

III. महाक्षेत्र Mahākṣetra — the great precinct · Tier II

𑀫𑀳𑀸𑀓𑁆𑀱𑁂𑀢𑁆𑀭  ·  𐨨𐨱𐨐𐨿𐨮𐨅𐨟𐨿𐨪

The same word as Sukhet, doing the opposite work. Kṣetra is a field; it is also a sacred precinct. In Sukhet it means the ground a man ploughs. Here it means the ground a temple owns.

Four reigns ago the crown made a grant — a दानक्षेत्र dānakṣetra, a granted field — of a hundred villages to the temple establishment of Mahākṣetra: the land, the revenue, and the right to judge. Nobody moved. The farmers stayed in their houses and went on ploughing the fields their grandfathers had ploughed. Only their standing changed. They are tenants now, on land their families cleared, and the rent goes to a precinct they may enter only by one gate.

They have not turned bandit. This is the part worth getting right. The answer here is not a gang but a song.

निम्बा Nimbā is a woman of no family, who sings in the vernacular rather than in Sanskrit, and what she sings is that the gods are not interested in grants, in birth, or in who may enter by which gate. She is not armed. She has no demands. The temple cannot answer her because its poets are not as good, and the crown cannot arrest her because she has done nothing, and every month there are more people in the field outside the precinct wall than there were the month before.

  • The dungeon is भूमिगृह Bhūmigṛha, the earth-house: the vaults beneath Mahākṣetra, where the grain of a hundred villages is stored against a bad year, and where the grant itself is kept on copper plates.
  • The decision. A party may read the plates, steal them, destroy them, or carry them to Songaṛh. Each does something different and none of them helps Nimbā, who does not want the plates and says so.

IV. कालवन Kālavana — the black wood · Tier III

𑀓𑀸𑀮𑀯𑀦  ·  𐨐𐨫𐨬𐨣

Kāla is black. Kāla is also time. The name has always been read both ways and both are correct.

A tract of burning-grounds — श्मशान śmaśāna — and the wood behind them, where the province’s dead are brought. The अग्निपाल agnipāla, the fire-keepers, live at अग्निघाट Agnighāṭ: hereditary, indispensable, and forbidden the streets of Songaṛh.

They are the exact counterpart of the nasusālār of Bagānrūd, and the echo is worth keeping. Two civilisations that agree on almost nothing have each produced a hereditary caste that handles the dead and may not come indoors — and in both regions they are the only people who know what is actually happening.

The horror is a bargain, not a concealment. In a fig tree at the edge of the burning-ground hangs a वेताल vetāla — a thing that occupies a corpse, head downward, and which talks. It cannot be fought in any useful sense. It answers questions, exactly and truthfully, and it names its price, and it has been doing both for longer than the province has existed.

The trouble is not the vetāla. The trouble is that people keep going to the tree, and have been for three generations, and every answer was paid for. The dungeon is the accumulated weight of what was paid: a chain of bargains, each one small and reasonable, which have compounded into something nobody intended and nobody can now unmake. A party’s work is to establish who asked what, and what they gave — and the last name on that list is one they will not want.

And the vetāla will tell them. For a price.

V. शतद्वार Śatadvāra — the Hundred Gates · Tier III–IV

𑀰𑀢𑀤𑁆𑀯𑀸𑀭  ·  𐨭𐨟𐨡𐨿𐨬𐨪

Four leagues up the Ujjvalā stands the capital of the Vārkava, empty for a thousand years: courts, cisterns, a processional way two miles long, and a hundred gates of which perhaps sixty can still be found.

Everybody goes there. Pilgrims, antiquarians, treasure-seekers, and once a year the Rājā, to hold a rite asserting that he is the Vārkava’s heir — a claim resting on nothing a court of law would look at twice.

The villagers who live among the stones call it सौदुआर Saudvār and do not connect it with the Śatadvāra of the chronicles. It is the same name, worn down through three tongues, and they have been saying it for forty generations.

Three things are in it.

  1. The Vārkava’s own records, in Brahmi, on stone — which the court cannot read fluently, because Brahmi is a monumental hand and Songaṛh’s pandits are trained on manuscripts. There are men in Timirāvana who can. They have not been asked.
  2. The tombs, and the dispute about whether the dynasty is in them.
  3. What is underneath. The Hundred Gates was not built on empty ground. There is an older footing beneath the processional way, laid by people who did not use these proportions, and the step-well at Sukhet has the same carving on it.

The spine of the whole region. Tier I ends at a carving nobody can read. Tier IV ends at the same carving, forty feet beneath an empire that did not mention it. In between, a party learns that every name in this province is a translation of an older name, and that the court at Songaṛh has renamed itself within living memory for precisely the same reason the Vārkava renamed everything a thousand years before. Payasvatī’s starting region is about inheritance, and about how little of it is yours.

The last row is the one that makes the two provinces feel different at a table. In Azade you win by producing a document. Here you win by knowing what a word used to mean.

VIII. For play

  • Tier I. Learn that हाथीगाँव and हत्थिगाम are one village. Go down the step-well. Come out with a rubbing nobody can read.
  • Tier II. Mahākṣetra. Decide what you think a grant is. Nimbā will not thank you for anything you do, and will not condemn you either, which is worse.
  • Tier III. Kālavana. Find out who has been going to the tree.
  • Tier III–IV. Śatadvāra, where the rubbing from Tier I matches a stone under the processional way — and where a party that has been to the Ukkul Hills can read both.
