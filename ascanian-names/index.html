Ascanian Names

The sound-divide, a gazetteer, and the naming of persons

Names marked † are already in the archive and are fixed. The rest are proposals.

Ascanian needs no conversion table, being written in the same letter as Thetch. What it has instead is a line across the middle of the realm, and every place-name in the country declares which side of it the place stands on.

I. The sound-divide

At some point in the deep past the speech of the south and the uplands shifted, and the speech of the north and the coast did not. Every word with p, t or k in it came out differently on the two sides, and the line where it stops has not moved since — running roughly east and west across the middle of the realm, through provinces, through dioceses and occasionally through villages.

So Marbeke and Marbach are the same name; Eikholt and Eichholz are the same wood; and on the Maren road there are two villages four miles apart, on either bank, called Nieuwdorp and Neudorf, which have quarrelled over a mill for two hundred years and have never once suspected that they are called the same thing.

A party can walk across a sound change in an afternoon. It is the Ascanian counterpart of हाथीगाँव and हत्थिगाम being one village, and it costs nothing to run.

Nobody in Ascania has a name for the line, because nobody in Ascania sees it as a line. The philologists of Brestan, in Thetland, call it die Lautscheide — the sound-divide — and outsiders noticing what a civilisation cannot see about itself is by now a habit of this setting.

Which resolves "Low Gothic"

The compendium has long carried an open question: Low Gothic names both a historical grouping of kingdoms and the trade koine of the northern ports, and the senses wanted separating.

They do not want separating. They are one word. Low is the country — the low country, the north and the coast, the far side of the divide — and Low Gothic is what is spoken there and what the kingdoms there are called. It is the same construction as the Low Countries.

It is the Gothic half that is the lie. See Ascania §V.

II. Gazetteer

Luden and the Northern Duchies — Wendish

Luden is not Ascanian. It is Wendish: a Slavonic duchy inside a Germanic realm, holding the only road east, and its Duke is one of the Seven who raise the High King. The relation is that of Pomerania, Poland or Bohemia to the Reich beyond them — subject in law, foreign in speech, and indispensable.

Every town here has two names, and the chancery uses the second.

Dubrava is in the archive already — as Дубрава, among the villages of the Eastern Kingdoms, eight hundred miles east and in another realm entirely. It is the same word and the same people. The political border cuts a language that does not notice it, exactly as the sound-divide cuts the Reich, one layer out.

And Ludenmark, in the Berglands, is the march against Luden — a German marcher district named for the Slavs beyond it, which it no longer is, the Slavs having been inside the realm for two hundred years and voting in its elections.

The literary register

The Northern Duchies keep the older literary tongue of the east — the formal, liturgical Wendish that the Eastern Kingdoms also use — and they write it in Cyrillic.

So the Ascanian chancery cannot read the charters of one of its own electors. Luden’s grants, judgments and title-deeds are in an alphabet nobody at Wendelstatt has learnt, and when a dispute reaches the Diet the Duke supplies the translation. He has never yet been caught supplying a convenient one, which is not the same as never having done it.

III. The naming of persons

This section is meant to be used. Anybody populating an Ascanian zone needs a hundred names before dinner, and the two tables below will coin them indefinitely without a die-roll looking like a die-roll.

Two layers, and most people carry both. The Germanic name comes from the family, and is inherited element by element. The Sarceran name comes from the font, the empire having left its Latin in the liturgy and the Church having gone on using it. A man entered in the font-book as Laurentius is Lorenz in the chancery, Lenz in his own village, and Wolfram to his mother, because that was his grandfather’s name. Which name he offers depends on who is asking, exactly as Songaṛh does in Payasvatī.

Why there is no third layer. The Ecclesia’s scripture is the Azakir, translated out of Anântêš. It carries no Hebrew and no Greek, and so there is no stratum of scriptural given names in Ascania at all — nothing answering to our Johns, Jameses, Marys and Peters, which in Europe swamped the native stock from the twelfth century and never gave it back. Ascania’s native stock was never swamped. The two-element Germanic names are still the ordinary names of ordinary people, which is why the ploughman at Wolfsbruch is Hartmut and not something out of a gospel. The test is the root, not the saint: admissible names are Germanic or Sarceran, and nothing else.

The Germanic stock — two elements, and how a house recombines them

A name is one element from the front list and one from the back, and a child’s name takes one element from each parent. That is the whole system, and it is why the same half-dozen syllables run through a lineage for four hundred years: Markward’s father was Wichmann, his mother Adelheid, and his daughter is Markgund or Adelhild as the family pleases.

The devout use native vocabulary, not imported names: Gotthart, Gottfried, Gottschalk — God’s servant — Gotthold, and for women Gottlinde. A family of that sort is pious in the Ascanian manner, which is to say in Ascanian.

The proportions are a map, as in Azade. Sarceran names are thick in Obana, in the free cities and among advocates and clerks — anybody whose business is conducted in writing — and thin to almost nothing in Hathing and the upland villages, where the font-name is entered once and never used again.

By rank

  • Nobility take von and a place: Markward von Harland, Adelheid von Rabenstein. A house that has lost its place keeps the von and the quarrel.
  • Burghers take a trade or a byname: Bracht, Kessler, Sattler, Weiss, Kurz, Vogt, Zimmer, Eisen, Schreiber, Furter, Gerber.
  • Incomers take -er from where they came: a man from Grünthal is Grünthaler, and his grandchildren still are, long after anybody remembers why.
  • In the low country the same byname takes the low shape: Becker in the uplands is Bakker on the coast, and the two are cousins who have not spoken since the mill business. The sound-divide runs through the surnames as well as the villages, and a man’s name will often place him within forty miles.
  • Gothic affectation. A house pressing its pedigree adopts an -ung plural — die Ermenungen — or an archaic given name in -ric, -mund or -mar. It is a reliable sign that the claim is recent.
  • In Luden the whole system is different, Luden being Wendish: given names and patronymics out of the Slavonic stock, and the chancery writing them down as best it can. See §II.

IV. The names that do not fit, and why that is useful

Two names in the archive are neither Low nor High. One of them is not a mystery at all, once one knows where to stand.

Baran, the seat of Luden, does not parse as Ascanian — and parses perfectly in Wendish, where it is simply the ram. It is not a fossil of some lost people; it is an ordinary word in the ordinary language of the duchy, and the only curious thing about it is that the chancery never translated it, as it translated Bielgrod and Czarnolas and all the rest. The lesson is worth keeping, because it will come up again on every border in the setting: a name that will not parse in one tongue may be the plainest word in the neighbour’s, and the scholar who cannot find the neighbour invents a vanished race instead. See §II.

Obana, in the Berglands, is the genuine outlier. It is Sarceran: a legion wintered there for eighty years and the district never stopped sounding like it — Albana, Ferraria, Vallis Obana. The Ascanians around it call the whole district Welsch, the foreigners' country, which is what Germanic speakers have always called the people who went on speaking Latin at them.

Both are worth protecting from tidying. A map where every name parses is a map with no history in it, and Ascania’s whole character is that its names record three conquests and one annexed duchy, none of which its nobility cares to discuss in those terms.

What is still wanting

  • Where the divide actually runs, town by town. The rule above decides every case; somebody has to draw the line on a map.
  • The five royal seats are placeholders — Swardenburg, Swyndeburg, Middeburg, Marenburg, Ermenstede are all formed on the region’s name, which real capitals usually are not.
  • No river-names. The Claret and the Marbeke are the only two in the archive, and a realm this size runs on rivers.
  • Obana’s Sarceran names are three, and the pocket would carry a dozen.
  • No Gothic sample. A charter of ten lines, in the real East Germanic shape, would make the whole pedigree business land.
  • No Azakir names. The scripture is Anântêš and nobody has yet drawn a name out of it. A small devout stratum would take them, as our own took Hebrew ones — and until the Anântêš phonotactics are settled there is nothing to take.
  • Luden’s own name-stock is a stub. The gazetteer is Wendish; the persons are not. Given names, patronymics and the chancery’s mangling of both want a section of their own, drawn from the same Slavonic stock as the Eastern Kingdoms so that the two match.
