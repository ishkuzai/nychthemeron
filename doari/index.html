Do'ari

Elves · hulden · taldar Children of the Heavens

They are more intelligent and possess a greater capacity for magic than any other mortal race; however, unlike the Kar’aqa, they are vulnerable to the maddening forces at play throughout the greater cosmos.

The Do’ari are the only living descendants of the Tal’daren, a small kingdom of ancient times. They have no country. They are scattered through the human kingdoms of Voralan, and they have made of that scattering a kind of profession: the Do’ari quarter is a fixture of every great city and most large towns of the central kingdoms, and the Do’ari who live in it are scholars, physicians, notaries, astronomers and the keepers of libraries their hosts cannot read.

Names

Hulden is what the Common Tongue calls them, out of Gothic folklore. Taldar is the formal and scholarly term, and covers more than the Do’ari alone: of the Taldar peoples there remain also the Nalasi and the Siel’a. Elf and faun are borrowings — the one from Gothic legend, the other from the woodland spirits of Sarceran and Anachoran myth. Do’ari is what they call themselves.

The distinction matters, because the Taldar at large are a forest people who are thought a myth by their neighbours and would rather remain one, concealing their inhuman marks and passing for men where the woods have been felled. The Do’ari are the branch that stopped concealing and moved into the city. Not all of them are content with the bargain: many of the rural Do’ari communities have been drifting back out of sophistication for some generations, the old forests pulling steadily at their minds.

Body and mind

Taller and fairer than men on average, and otherwise not unlike them save for a tail of some two feet, cow-like, covered in short soft fur from light brown to black; some have pointed ears, and the canines are usually prominent. Skin runs from pale to tan with the latitude, hair from blonde to black, the eyes most often emerald, though blue, brown, amber and even gold occur. They carry less body hair than men, though the males may grow beards to rival a dwarf’s.

They are stronger and more robust than men, though weaker than the Hyn’ral, and they lack the dwarf’s endurance. What they have instead is regeneration, the inheritance of all Derathi descendants: they recover from wounds at near twice the speed of other mortals. A dwarf will outlast a Do’ari in one long battle; a Do’ari could fight small skirmishes for ever. The same faculty prolongs their lives, and a Do’ari some hundreds of years old is unremarkable.

Expansive Mind. Wisdom increased by a twentieth. Scholarly Upbringing. Those raised in human cities spend their lives among old books, and their Intellect is increased by a twentieth accordingly.

Base attributes. Strength 9 · Stamina 11 · Resilience 9 · Agility 13 · Wisdom 15 · Intellect 15 · Spirit 14 · Perception 15 · Charisma 9. Their variability is 3, among the lowest of any race: the Do’ari differ from one another less than other peoples do.

A note for the system. These figures stand well above both the human and the dwarven tables. If the two powers are to be balanced race against race, either the Do’ari numbers come down or the others go up. Flagged, not resolved.

Faith and magic

They have the greatest native capacity for sorcery of any mortal people and the least protection against what sorcery shows them. The Kar’aqa can read the cosmic and astral lore without harm because they do not care what it means; the Do’ari care intensely, and it is the caring that undoes them. A Do’ari scholar who has gone too far is one of the standard tragedies of the setting.

In matters of faith they are conformists in the city and something else in the countryside. The urban Do’ari attend the Light of the Heavens, hold office in its institutions, and are over-represented among its canon lawyers. What the drifting village communities do in the woods is not asked about by their neighbours and not volunteered by them.

In the war

The Do’ari are not fighting, and are extremely interested. They supply the Eastern Kingdoms with cartographers, interpreters and physicians, and they supply themselves with intelligence. A Do’ari in Tucania is there to see what is in the forest before the Empire burns it down, and the most likely first contact between the two powers' scholars — Do’ari and Kar’aqa, the two deepest learnings in the world — will happen on that frontier, in a tent, with armies a day’s march away.

Playing one

The mage, the scholar and the outsider-within. The characteristic Do’ari problem is not persecution but conditionality: everything the Do’ari have in the human kingdoms is held on terms. The characteristic Do’ari temptation is the book nobody else can read.

Sanity is the real resource for this character. The Do’ari can learn anything and cannot survive all of it.

What is still wanting

  • Nalasi and Siel’a are names only. Two surviving Taldar peoples with nothing behind them.
  • The relation to the elves of the far east is undrawn. The empire of On’Yezdo divides into High Elves on Yezdo itself and Low Elves on the mainland north of it, with its own names, towns and sword-types; whether these are Do’ari, another Taldar branch, or something else is not stated anywhere.
  • The Tal’daren kingdom is the hinge of the whole people and has three lines about it.
  • The Taldar collision remains open: the region of the Talud, the elven race, and the mythicised Taldoren who taught men the alphabet all stand on one root.
