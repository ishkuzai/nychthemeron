Human

Men · the race of men Children of the Heavens

No matter how savage an environment, the race of men will eventually seek to conquer it, even if attempting such will result in their untimely deaths.

Men are the most numerous people of the Old World and the least governable. They are second only to the Tulkata in the variety of their cultures, and first by a long way in the variety of their opinions about one another. Every institution the Children of the Heavens possess was built by men, and every schism in it was made by them also.

Body and mind

Men are the measure against which the archive describes everything else, which is to say that they are unremarkable in every particular and formidable in the aggregate. They are not so strong as the Hyn’ral, nor so quick as the Do’ari, nor so hardy as the Tulkata; they do not live long; they lose their wits readily in the presence of the cosmic and the astral. Against all this they set a breeding rate, a tolerance of discomfort, and an obstinacy which the setting’s own documents dignify with the name of a racial trait.

Denial. A man will march on dehydrated, feverish, bleeding, or three days past the point at which any other creature would have lain down, sustained entirely by a refusal to admit the position. The reckoning comes afterwards, and comes harder for having been deferred. In play this staggers environmental effects and gives a small chance of staggering a killing blow for sixty seconds; it does not prevent anything, it only postpones it.

Base attributes. Strength 8 · Stamina 8 · Resilience 8 · Agility 9 · Wisdom 10 · Perception 10 · Charisma 10. Intellect and Spirit are not recorded and are presumed to be 10, men being the baseline. Note that the attested Do’ari figures stand far above these; see the balance.

The nations

There is no such thing as a human polity in general. The important groupings are these.

The Voraic west. The old Gothic settlement of the continent, now the kingdoms of Ascania, Thetland, Darde, Berland, Lythe and Ancora, bound loosely by the Council of Kings and firmly by the Church. They speak Thetch, or a cousin of it, and reckon themselves the civilised world. Berland on its south-western peninsula is the oldest seat and the most isolated, cut off by the Stanfell Mountains; the Eowans who hold it came some fifteen hundred years ago and absorbed the Tuthollians who were there before.

The Eastern Kingdoms. The Great Empire in the East, risen within the last few hundred years and now holding a territory as large as the whole of Vora: Naderland, Obylen, Severia, the central farmlands and the Tucanian wilderness. Its people are Wends; its cities — Krasnitz, Rysburg, Ladenburg, Berce, Laske, Vorana — go back to heathen times, and several of its noble houses are older than the Empire that commands them. It received the Light of the Heavens early and fiercely, by way of devout missionaries from the west whose descendants are the Cathars, and it has lately begun naming its towns in the Ascanian manner in the hope of a seat at the Council. It is the Empire’s Great Settlement of Tucania that has started the only war now being fought between the two powers.

The Khanar and the steppe. South-east of the Empire’s central region, where the forests break into plain and steppeland, under the Hurdagar family at Rundalan. They are held to be distant kin of the Kunnic peoples of Cunderland, far away north of Thetland — a relation nobody in either place is pleased to be reminded of.

The eastern mountain kingdoms. Ulut Qayan, Arjatha and Khargara, reckoned distant Khanar kin; and Tsevrokh and Herran Thagal, the oldest cultures in the region, deep in the mountains and largely impervious to the outside world. These are the easternmost human civilisations known, and their folktales remember a war with demons in forgotten ages.

The south and the sea. The Kothari, the caravan peoples, Urama upon the northern shore of the Daracian Sea, and the maritime nations whose business carries them past both powers without much troubling to choose between them.

Faith

The Light of the Heavens is the faith of men above all, and the Ecclesia is a human institution staffed by human clerks. Where the faith has gone east it has gone sterner, not milder. The Cathars are the residue of that, and the Empire’s particular temper — austere, literal, suspicious of the comfortable western clergy — is the long consequence of having been converted by enthusiasts.

Mysticism is another matter, and in most human kingdoms a dangerous one. A man who pursues it does so against law, custom or both, and under names — witch, warlock, occultist — which are accusations as often as descriptions.

In the war

Men supply the manpower, the administration and the casus belli. The Great Settlement is a human policy, drawn up by human surveyors, and the stockades going up along the Tucanian rivers are garrisoned by men who have been told they are improving an empty province. Few of them have met a Tulkata. Almost none of them have met a Hadur.

Playing one

The default, and the one the setting is written from. A human character begins inside an institution — a kingdom, a charter company, an order, a parish — and the interesting play comes from what happens when the institution’s account of the frontier turns out to be false. Denial is a trait for a character who will not turn back.

What is still wanting

  • The Empire has no Emperor. No name, no dynasty, no capital is given; the central cities are listed without a seat among them being named chief.
  • The Great Settlement has no date and no charter, though both would be useful at a table.
  • The human peoples of the archive run to several dozen names with little behind most of them; the Voraic list wants pruning to those that will bear weight.
