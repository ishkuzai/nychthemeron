Vedan Names

An onomasticon for Trisindhu — five layers of speech in one country, and the three hands they are written in

Names marked † are already in the archive and are fixed. Every Sanskrit and Prakrit form below is given three times: in देवनागरी, as a reader of the wiki will meet it; in Brahmi, as it stands cut into stone; and in Kharosthi, as it is scrawled on bark and leather in the north-west. The romanisation is kept only so that the descent of one form from another may be followed.

All of it is generated by tools/scripts.py and tools/build_names.py; no glyph in this file was typed by hand.

I. The three hands

WhereWhat it isRuns
Brahmithe core and the southan abugida, cut for monuments and royal grantsleft to right
Kharosthithe north-west — Toyāvarta, and the Azadean roadan abugida out of an Aramaic abjad, written quickly, for bark and hideright to left
देवनागरीeverywhere, nowthe present hand, and the one the living tongue is set inleft to right

Kharosthi came up the road from Azade, as its direction of writing confesses, and it belongs to the kingdom at that end of the country. It makes no distinction between a long vowel and a short one, so a name written in it looks shorter and plainer than the same name cut in Brahmi — which is why Toyāvarta’s clerks and Kanakadeśa’s pandits have spelt the same towns differently for eight hundred years and each think the other careless.

A monumental grant in Brahmi, a merchant’s tally in Kharosthi and a tax register in Devanagari may all name one village and look like nothing to do with one another. That is the single most useful fact on this page for a table.

The cluster -st- doubles to -tth- and then simplifies with the vowel before it drawn long; -ग्राम loses its r, then its final vowel, and nasalises. A reader who has that by heart can date any name on the map in a moment.

III. What the archive’s own names turn out to be

The four towns and one lake the archive gives for the north-western kingdom are, between them, one from each of the five layers. Luck rather than design, but worth keeping.

And one quiet embarrassment

सुवर्णगढ़ Suvarṇagaṛh is not an ancient name. Its head, -गढ़, is the modern form; the ancient would be दुर्ग. The town was सोनगढ़ Songaṛh, and the court re-cut the first element into Sanskrit to sound as old as its pretensions require. The common people still say Songaṛh.

The kingdom that claims the Vaidana inheritance has a modern capital in a learned disguise — the same manoeuvre Zarestan performed upon Engonia, at the other end of the world, and neither court would thank anyone for drawing the comparison.

तिमिरावन · Timirāvana — the dark-river forest

The inversion worth keeping. This kingdom is older than Kanakadeśa and its chronicles are in Prakrit — so most of its towns bear Prakrit names with no Sanskrit ancestor at all. The Sanskrit forms in Kanakadeśan geographies are back-formations, invented by scholars who cannot believe a place-name might lack a classical original.

बंदर bandar is the same word as the -bandar of Azadean Nabandar. The two civilisations share a harbour vocabulary and have not remarked upon it, the men who keep the ledgers in either place being disinclined to etymology.

VI. The Ukkal layer

The substrate, and the oldest thing on the map. Short words, ending in a consonant or in -ur or -ol, carrying clusters no Indic tongue permits at that position. Ukkali is also where the heavy retroflex series of the southern vernaculars comes from.

It has no script of its own. What survives was written down by Indic scribes, in Brahmi, forced into an abugida that cannot spell it — which is why the forms disagree from one grant to the next.

The Azadeans took the vernacular, not the book. Their name for this country is Trihind, colloquially Hind — and the s which is स here is ه there, regularly, right across the vocabulary, which is the signature of the family both belong to. But it is the shape of तीसिंध Tīsindh the Azadean form answers to, not the शास्त्र-form त्रिसिन्धु. The name travelled by caravan and not by library.

VIII. Coining more

  1. Decide the century first, then the name. The layer is the whole method.
  2. Run every new Sanskrit name down the chain and record the vulgar form too. Half the country’s towns are known by two names, and the difference is who is speaking.
  3. -गंज means the last two or three generations. Never give it to anything older.
  4. -गढ़ is Hindustani, दुर्ग is Sanskrit. A Sanskrit first element on a -गढ़ is not an error; it is a court trying to look old, and should be used on purpose.
  5. Timirāvana’s names are Prakrit and stay Prakrit.
  6. Let Ukkali refuse the system, as Kafestan’s hill-names refuse the Azadean one.
  7. Choose the hand to suit the object. Brahmi for a grant, a boundary stone or a temple lintel; Kharosthi for a letter, a tally or anything from Toyāvarta; Devanagari for anything a living person is reading.

What is still wanting

  • The Ukkal list is seven elements and ought to be forty, drawn to a settled phonotactics as the Orthrus engine draws its roots. This discharges open question 8 only in part.
  • No Prakrit is named. There should be two or three — that of the Ujjvalā and that of the Timirā will not have been the same — and Timirāvana’s chronicles are in one of them.
  • Vaidana has no king-list, and Śatadvāra no date of ruin.
  • The Kar’aqa tongue is tonal and no Indic script can write it. What Timirāvana actually does about this — a diacritic system, a separate convention, or simply giving up — is undecided, and the kingdom has three hundred years of almanacks that must have solved it.
