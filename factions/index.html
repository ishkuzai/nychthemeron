The Two Powers

The Children of the Heavens and the Children of the Wilds

There are two major powers in the world, each with three prominent races. Any neutral character or faction must try to meander through the endless conflicts and rivalries which seek to consume the world as a whole.

— Introduction to the Project, the governing statement

The division is not a treaty and cannot be dissolved by one. It is older than any kingdom now standing, and the quarrel proceeds, as the same document has it, not simply from ideological outlook but from the very beings of those who hold it. All six peoples are children of one source. Some of them, according to the one party, were afterwards twisted and warped to serve the vision of false and ancient gods; according to the other, some were coaxed out of the world that bore them and taught to despise their own mother. Neither account can be settled by argument, which is why the thing has lasted.

One power finds its reverence in the heavens, and in the light of its cosmic lord and his servants, and would see all Creation ordered and tamed. The other finds its truth in nature itself, and gives its devotion to countless wild gods and to a mysticism the first power has declared abominable.

The theatre of all this is Endaran, as the elves name it, or, as seafaring men prefer to say, the Old World.

I. The Children of the Heavens

The civilised races, who stand for order and progress, and who bend the world to their will, shaping and changing it as they see fit.

What holds them together is not blood, for they share none, but a settled account of what the world is for. The heavens are ordered; the heavens are therefore right; what is ordered after their pattern is good, and what resists ordering is at best raw material and at worst an enemy. From this follow the road, the charter, the drained marsh, the surveyed forest, the written law and the cathedral — and also the conviction, held without embarrassment in every chancery from Brestan to Vorana, that a wilderness with people in it is still a wilderness.

Their faith is the Light of the Heavens, carried east in the early Middle Ages by devout men who thought the gospel in the west had grown soft, and whose descendants are the Cathars. Their diplomacy is the Council of Kings. Their weakness is that they agree about Creation and about nothing else: Ancora is in civil war, Lythe has not finished subduing its own clans, the dwarves of Haldorak have not spoken in two thousand years, and the Great Empire in the East is a petitioner at the Council rather than a member of it.

II. The Children of the Wilds

The savage races, who live in tune with the natural world, standing for balance and existing in harmony with the untamed wilderness.

Savage is the Ecclesia’s word and is worth distrusting. The Tulkata keep an empire with provinces and a chancery; the Kar’aqa hold a body of cosmic and astral learning to rival the Do’ari; and the Hadur, of the three, are the only ones who can show the scar of how they were made. What the three have in common is a refusal of the first premise — that the world is unfinished and wants mending. They hold, on the contrary, that it was finished long ago, that the heavens are merely the roof of it, and that a thing which must be tamed before it can be loved was never loved at all.

Their religion is not one religion. It is the adwa of the southern trolls, the spirits and eldritch deities of the Hadur shamans, the moon they call the primal wanderer, and whatever it is the Kar’aqa read in the dark without going mad. What the Church sees when it looks at all this is a single abomination with several faces, and that misreading is itself one of the engines of the war.

Their weakness is the mirror of the Heavens': they are not an alliance but an agreement not to interfere, and it has never yet survived a season of real pressure.

III. Neither

Many people of every race choose to be neutral — for trade, for adventure, or from conviction — and some whole peoples are known for it. The gnomes keep out of it and keep their ears open; the giants are not organised enough to join anything; the fishmen are not asked. Between the two powers lies a great deal of the world in which the quarrel is carried on by merchants, missionaries and lawyers, and never once by armies.

IV. Where the war actually is

This is the part most easily got wrong. The two powers are universal; the war is not. There is exactly one place where they are presently killing one another in numbers, and it is the north-east of the continent.

The Great Empire in the East — the Eastern Kingdoms, a Wendish power risen within the last few hundred years — holds a territory as large as the whole of Vora and scarcely peopled: from Naderland in the north-west, which touches Ascania’s Duchy of Luden by a single trade-road through the Haldoracian Valley, by way of Obylen in the Urthan mountains and coastal Severia, to the vast eastern wilderness of Tucania. It is ruled from the central cities — Krasnitz, Rysburg, Ladenburg, Berce, Laske, Vorana — and it wants a seat at the Council of Kings badly enough to have begun naming its towns in the Ascanian manner.

The Empire has lately resolved upon the Great Settlement: a programme of founding towns throughout Tucania and opening its timber, iron, furs and waterways. In the chanceries this is not a conquest. It is the improvement of an empty province.

Tucania is not empty. It is the western reach of the Tulkata Empire, and the particular realm whose forests and lakes the surveyors have begun marking out is Svelekebá, the bear-wood — one kingdom of the Tulkata and by no means the whole of them. Svelekebá has taken into its pay, or into its confidence, three Hadur clans off the northern steppe, who bring horse, shamans, and an old and specific grievance against anything that builds a wall.

So the war has a shape a table of players can actually hold:

  • It is a frontier war, fought over surveyed lines, fortified river-crossings, burnt stockades and one road.
  • It is not sanctioned from above on either side. The Tulkata Emperor has not declared for Svelekebá, and the Council of Kings has not declared for an empire that is not a member of it. Both may yet, and that is the clock the campaign runs on.
  • It is deniable. The Empire calls it banditry. Svelekebá calls it hunting.
  • It will not stay small. Obylen’s mines are dwarf-worked, and a dwarven death in a raid brings Haltelan in. Naderland’s road is Ascania’s road. The Kar’aqa have begun sending observers, and nobody sends an observer to a war he means to stay out of.

Everywhere else, for the present, the two powers touch without blood: in the southern markets of Gantumba, in the Do’ari quarter of every Voraic city, on the long shore of the Daracian Sea. That is by design. A world wholly at war is not a setting but a battlefield, and there is nothing to do in it but fight.

The pairings are deliberate and should be kept in anything written hereafter. Dwarf against goatman is the stone wall against the thing that will not stand still. Elf against ravenman is two scholarships of equal depth, the one keeping its sanity by not looking and the other by not caring. Man against troll is the only pairing that is simply like against like, which is why those two do most of the dying.

VI. What each side is wrong about

The Heavens are wrong that the Wilds are one thing. They are three peoples with nothing in common but a grievance, and the Church’s insistence on treating them as a single abomination has done more to unite them than any troll chancery ever managed.

The Wilds are wrong that the Heavens can be waited out. The settled races do not tire, and the Great Settlement is not an expedition but a policy. A generation of patience is a generation of surveyors.

Both are wrong about the Hadur, who were made by a servant of the Demon Lord Marach out of men, and who are therefore not a wild people at all but an injured one; whose place among the Wilds is a matter of hospitality rather than of nature, and could be lost.

The natural first campaign is the Great Settlement itself: a surveying party going east into Tucania with a charter, a priest, a dwarven engineer and an escort, into a forest that is inhabited, claimed, and older than the charter. Nobody in the party need be a villain for it to end in blood.

What is still wanting

  • Svelekebá is a working name, built from the attested Uzda words for bear and forest. The kingdom is required by the structure; the name is the author’s to settle.
  • The three Hadur clans have no names, nor has any Hadur anywhere in the archive.
  • The Tulkata Emperor has no name, seat or title. The southern trolls have a full apparatus — P’acha ti Kuen ti Mabu, Fadu ti Fada, King of Kings — and the eastern empire has none.
  • Two statements in the archive disagree. Introduction to the Project makes the Tulkata widespread throughout the Old World; the Lore Overview makes the Fakal the only trolls left in it. The likeliest reconciliation is that Tucania and the lands beyond it are reckoned outside the Old World proper — but it wants deciding rather than assuming.
  • The Uzda tongues are here taken to be Tulkata speech, and the Cruntish tongues southern troll speech. The archive files both under "troll languages" and says no more.
  • The southern axis is not drawn here. Gantumba, the Fakal kingdoms, the Seren and the wall belong to the same two powers but to a different quarrel, and should have a page of their own.
