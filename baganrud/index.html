بگانرود · Bagānrūd

A province of Bagad, and the first country a party should see

Five areas about one city: a gentle one to learn in, a poor one with a grievance, a frightening one, and a frontier. The shape is deliberately the shape of a well-built starting region — hub, pastoral, injustice, horror, siege — because that arrangement works. Everything inside the shape is Iranian, and the result is not a western march with Persian names painted on it.

It lies in Bagad, west of Daracia, where the settled country gives out into the Eborian wildlands: the one province of Azade that has a priesthood, a classical past and a dangerous border all within three days' ride.

I. رودآباد Rudabad — the city

The river-settlement, which is what its name says, on the رود بگان Rūd-e Bagān; and above it on the rock, آذربگان Āẕarbagān, the eldest fire in the world, which is the reason there is a city here at all and not a village.

What a party finds: the bazaar; the دیوان dīvān, the governor’s chancery, where every dispute in the province is written down twice; the موبدان mōbedān, the priesthood, who are also the province’s sorcerers; the caravanserai on the Vora road; and a quarter of narrow straight streets laid out by somebody who was not an Azadean, where the old families still bear names ending in -us and nobody has asked why for four hundred years.

The register. Sorcery in Azade is lawful, examined and recorded. A character who wants to work magic is examined at the lesser fire-house, pays a fee, and has his name written into the Register of the Fire. This is a plot device of the first order. The Ecclesia of the north would give a great deal for a copy of that register; it is a list, by name and village, of every licensed sorcerer in the province, kept in a building with one door.

II. باغستان Bāghestān — the garden country · Tier I

Walled gardens, orchards, vineyards and melon-fields, every one of them fed from below. The water comes by کاریز kārīz — underground channels driven for miles from the foot of the hills, so that nothing is lost to the sun. A man who maintains them is a مقنی moqannī, and he goes down with a lamp and a rope and his neighbours holding the other end.

Towns: چهارباغ Chahārbāgh, four gardens; and کاریزان Kārizān, the place of the channels. The examination is sat at the آتشکده خرد Ātashkade-ye Khord, the lesser fire-house.

The small troubles are jackals, orchard-thieves, a dispute over a wall, and Golnāz, the parī of the spring, who is not malevolent and is not safe.

And then the channels. Something has got into the کاریز کهنه Kārīz-e Kohne, the old channel, and two moqannī have not come up. Miles of worked stone, waist-deep water, no room to swing a sword and nowhere to turn round. It occupies the same place in the design as a goblin warren and has nothing whatever in common with one.

III. خشکرود Xoshkrud — the dry river · Tier II

A plain that was irrigated within living memory and is not now.

The great channel was dug by a levy — every village sending men for a season, for years. When it ran, the water-rights were apportioned: so much to the fire, so much to the great houses, so much to the towns, and what was left to the tail of the channel. There has not been anything left for six years. The men who dug it are the men with no water.

They are the کاریزکنان Kārizkanān, the channel-diggers, and they are on the road. Not quite a bandit gang: a guild that has stopped pretending it is one. Their leader is Dādmihr, who was the district’s میراب mīrāb — the water-master, the official who apportions the flow and records it — and who therefore knows precisely who took what, and has the ledgers.

Town: بند شکسته Band-e Shekaste, the broken weir, which is not broken by accident.

The chain ends at سربند Sarband, the head-works: a buried sluice-house where the water is actually divided, held by men who are being paid by somebody in Rudabad. A party may restore the flow, or seize it, or sell what Dādmihr knows. The governor would rather they did none of the three, and will say so politely.

IV. دخمگان Dakhmagān — the towers · Tier III

A dry tableland of دخمه daxma — towers of silence, where the dead are laid out for the birds, because earth and fire and water must not be fouled by a corpse.

The horror here is not that the dead rise. It is نسو nasu, the corpse-druj: the thing that enters a body at the moment of death and spreads from it to whatever touches it unpurified. The fear is not of being killed. It is of carrying something home on your hands, and of what your own village will do to you if they think you have.

Town: پاکرود Pākrud, the clean river, where the نسوسالار nasusālār live — the corpse-bearers, hereditary, indispensable, and forbidden to enter any other town in the province. They are the only people who know what is happening, and nobody will sit down with them.

What is happening is that the rite has not been properly performed for a year, and Burzēn, the district’s mōbed, has concealed it. There were too many dead in the bad winter, too few bearers, and not enough birds. Everything in the district follows from that one piece of cowardice, and the dungeon — دخمه سیاه Daxma-ye Siyāh, the black tower — is simply where the consequences have accumulated.

V. پیشمرز Pīshmarz — the fore-march · Tier III–IV

Forest and marsh, where the cleared land stops and سیاهبیشه Siyāhbīshe, the black thicket, begins. Beyond it is Eboria: the country the Sarceran Empire spent twenty years and a third of its treasury failing to take, and then walled off instead.

Town: دژ نیو Dez-e Nev, the fort of the brave, holding the ford and the road, under Rāmbed, who has two-thirds of the garrison he is supposed to have because Jangestan has the Empire’s attention and this border does not.

What comes out of the thicket: dīvs of several kinds, none of them catalogued; Eborian men who are not entirely men; and, once in a generation, something that was made when Orobas held that country and has been asleep since he fell.

  • برج سوخته Borj-e Sūkhte — the burnt tower, a Sarceran watch-post from the years when the empire’s reach ended at this river. Somebody has been living in it.
  • گورآب Gōrāb — the grave-water, a marsh with an older thing in it than anything in the thicket.
  • شکارگاه Shekārgāh — the governor’s hunting preserve, which the garrison resents being forbidden, there being deer in it and short rations in the fort.

The ledger is the thing to hold on to. In this province, almost every quest ends with a document: the register at the fire-house, the mīrāb's water-rolls, the governor’s double-entered chancery, the mōbed's falsified record of rites performed. A party that learns to read Azadean has a second class of weapon.

VIII. For play

  • Tier I — Bāghestān. Be examined, be registered, go down the old channel. A party leaves with a licence and a reputation in one village.
  • Tier II — Xoshkrud. Meet Dādmihr. Decide, fairly early, whether the law of this province is worth defending. Everything downstream turns on that answer.
  • Tier III — Dakhmagān, or Pīshmarz, in either order. One is a horror with a man at the bottom of it; the other is a siege with nothing human at the bottom of it at all.
  • The province entire. If the party restored the water, Xoshkrud feeds Dakhmagān’s bearers and Pīshmarz’s garrison, and both go better. If they sold it, both go worse. The region is built so that the Tier II decision is still being paid for at Tier IV.
