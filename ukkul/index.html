The Ukkul

उक्कल · the people before the Pavasians · their tongue, and the one book of it that survives

The Ukkul held the three rivers before anybody now living held anything. They are not Indo-European, not related to the Payasvatī tongues or to any other speech in the world, and they have been almost wholly absorbed. Almost.

Their own name for themselves is उक्क् ukk, we, the people — which is what a people call themselves when they have not yet met anybody they need to be distinguished from.

I. The evidence that they existed

Linguistic, and overwhelming.

  • The river-names तिम्र timra and गबिर् gabir are not Sanskrit. They are Ukkul, re-cut by folk etymology into तिमिरा the dark and गभीरा the deep, which they do not mean. Timra is the winding one; gabir is the swallower, which is what a delta does.
  • तोयकान Toyacan does not parse as Sanskrit either; its first element has been pulled toward तोय, water, which is what the lake is.
  • The southern Payasvatī vernaculars carry words with no Indo-European cousins — for rice, fish, boat, crocodile, net and the monsoon — and a retroflex series the northern dialects cannot account for. That series is Ukkul, and so is a good deal of what sounds most characteristic about southern speech.

Hold-outs remain in two places: the उक्कलगिरि, the Ukkul Hills between Timirāvana and the sea, where some dozens of villages still speak it; and the marshes of the Gabhīrā delta, where the boat-people are reckoned Ukkul by everybody except themselves.

II. The tongue

No aspirates, no voiced–voiceless contrast worth the name in the dentals, a fondness for doubled consonants and for the clusters -mb-, -ṅg-, -nd-, -ṇḍ-, and a retroflex series heavier than any Indic tongue. Roots are of one or two syllables and end in a consonant far more often than not, which is why an Ukkul name set in Devanagari acquires a virama at the end that no Pavasian reader pronounces.

The script does not fit the speech, and the places where it fails are worth knowing:

So the recitation at court and the pronunciation in the hills are different performances of the same word, and the hill version is the right one.

Making words

  • -ur — place of
  • -ol — place of (smaller)
  • -aṇ — one who
  • -kaṭṭ — settlement
  • -in — of, belonging to
  • -ki — plural
  • -mor — song, the telling of
  • -tai — great, honoured

So पिर् pir, a chief, gives पिरुर् pirur, a chief’s seat; गुळ् guḻ, a slave, gives गुळ्कि guḻki, the taken; and कोत् kot, to remember, gives कोतण् Kotaṇ, the one who remembers — upon which everything else in this folder depends.

III. What survives

One poem. See Kotaṇmor, the Song of Kotaṇ, which is the national epic of Payasvatī and was not composed by anybody Payasvatī descends from.

Besides it: the river-names, the retroflexes, perhaps two hundred words in the southern vernaculars, the hill villages, the delta boat-people, and the name of a ford.

What is still wanting

  • No Ukkul grammar beyond the eight affixes above. Case, number and the verb are undone.
  • No second text. A law, a genealogy or a boundary-stone inscription would change the region’s history from a reconstruction into a record.
  • Whether the hill Ukkul and the delta boat-people speak one tongue or two. Eight hundred miles apart and no contact for a thousand years says two.
  • What the Ukkul called the Do’ari, who are their one attested ally.
