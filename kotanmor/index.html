Kotaṇmor

कोतण्मोर् — the Song of Kotaṇ · in seven बाश्ki, "roads"

The national epic of Payasvatī, recited at every court from Songaṛh to Mīnadvāra, taught to children, quoted in judgments, and painted on temple walls.

It is not a Pavasian poem. It is Ukkul, it is older than the Pavasian settlement by an unknown stretch of time, and it was already old when the Pavasians took the country and took the poem with it. Every name in it is in a language that has been dead in the lowlands for a thousand years, and nobody at court can parse a syllable of it — which has not prevented anybody from loving it, any more than a Voraic schoolboy is troubled by what Agamemnon means.

The joke the whole thing rests on. The heroes of Payasvatī’s national epic are the people the Payasvatīs conquered, and the poem is about a war their own ancestors had no part in. The names say so. Nobody can read the names.

I. The argument

In the time before the rivers were counted, the उक्क् ukk held तम्बोर् tambor, the Great Water, and the Do’ari held the forests east of it.

A power rose in the south, beyond the Great Ford, and came north — not for the country, which it did not want, but for people, whom it took away in thousands. Among those taken were the whole of one village, and among those left was a cowherd of no family the poem thinks worth recording, named कोतण् kotaṇ.

What follows is not a war. It is a rescue, conducted by too few men, and the poem is quite clear that it should not have worked.

II. The cast

Parses as
कोतण् Kotaṇkot + -aṇthe Rememberer. The hero, and a cowherd of no family worth the poem’s notice
कङण् Kaṅaṇkaṅ + -aṇthe Forgetter. His companion, who takes the troll king’s price
तन्वित् Tanvittanduṇ + vitoath-brother. The one who does not turn
टलुड्व Ṭaluḍva—the troll king. Not an Ukkul name: a mangling of something Cruntish, perhaps Chal’adwa, tiger-of-the-spirits
गुळ्कि Guḻkiguḻ + -kithe Taken. The captives, who are the poem’s true subject

And the Do’ari, whose names are in an eastern dialect and stand out in the poem’s mouth as plainly as a foreign word in a hymn:

Urzailord of Mindo, who joins late and for his own reasons
Kailohis archer
LhansoiUrzai’s sword, named as Do’ari swords are

III. The places

Parses as

किल्लुश् Killuškil + lušthe Stone Ford. See below
कल्मुल्लुङ् Kalmulluṅkal + mulluṅthe South Town — the troll city, which the poem never names otherwise, the Ukkul knowing only the direction of it
युसुर् Yusuryus + -urthe place of return, where the survivors settled
मिन्दो Mindo—the Blackwood. Eastern Do’ari, not Ukkul

IV. The two names that carry the poem

कोतण् Kotaṇ is the Rememberer. कङण् Kaṅaṇ is the Forgetter. They set out together; one of them takes the troll king’s price and the other does not; and the whole poem is an argument between remembering and forgetting conducted in the names of two men.

No Pavasian has ever noticed. To a pandit of Songaṛh these are two heroic names of the elder time, pleasingly alike in sound, as Kotan and Kangan. To a village in the Ukkul Hills they are a sentence.

This is the single most useful thing on the page. A party that carries a line of the epic into the hills and asks what it means will be told, and the poem they have known since childhood will turn over in their hands.

V. Killuš, and what the Pavasians did with it

The crossing of the poem is किल्लुश् Killuš — kil, stone, and luš, ford: the Stone Ford. It is where Kotaṇ goes south and where the Taken come back.

अश्मघाट Aśmaghāṭ means stone ford. It is a literal Sanskrit translation of the Ukkul name, made when the Pavasians took the country and kept the place.

And Aśmaghāṭ is where the Vedan kings broke the First Great Conquest twelve hundred years ago, in the one victory the whole region reckons its own — the only time a human power has beaten a Great Conquest in the field.

So: the proudest day in Payasvatī’s history was won at a ford whose name its people translated from a people they had displaced, in a war against trolls out of the south, repeating a war against trolls out of the south that is the subject of a poem they inherited from the displaced. Nobody has put this together, because doing so requires a man who knows both the epic and the Ukkul tongue, and such men live in hill villages and are not consulted about history.

VI. The seven roads

The poem is divided into बाश्ki, roads, which is also what it is about.

The first roadthe taking, and the village emptied
The second roadKotaṇ and Kaṅaṇ go east to the Do’ari, and are refused
The third roadwhy Urzai changes his mind, which the poem declines to explain
The fourth roadthe crossing at Killuš
The fifth roadKalmulluṅ, and the price Kaṅaṇ takes
The sixth roadthe loosing of the Taken
The seventh roadthe return, and Yusur; and the counting of who did not come back

The seventh is the longest, and is a list of names. Payasvatī recites it at funerals and does not know that it is doing so.

VII. For play

  • A line of the epic, and a hill village. The cheapest and best hook in the region.
  • A Fakal scholar is the only person alive who could identify Ṭaluḍva — the name is a mangled Cruntish one, and the southern trolls keep records.
  • The seventh road is a list of the dead, and somebody’s ancestor is on it.
  • The Order of the Golden Sun would find the poem’s sympathies alarming if anybody translated it.

What is still wanting

  • The poem has no verse form. Metre, line-length and whether it is sung or chanted are all undecided, and the Ukkul stress-pattern would settle them.
  • Urzai’s reason is the hole at the centre of the third road and should stay a hole, but the author ought to know what it is.
  • No text. Twenty lines of it, in Ukkul with a Devanagari gloss, would be worth more than this whole page.
  • Where Kalmulluṅ was, and whether anything is there now.
