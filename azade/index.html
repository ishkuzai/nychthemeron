Azade

Āzādšahr · the dominion of the free · the Ardavan realm

Azade lies south-east of Vora and north-east of the Whispering Sands, borders the Daracian Sea, and holds the main trade route with the far east. It is the pivot of the whole southern half of the setting: the only lawful magical learning in the world is taught there, the Church of the north was born there, and everything that passes between Vora and Trisindhu passes through it.

It is also not one power, and never has been for long.

I. The name

Keep it. Āzāde means the free, or the noble, in its own tongue, which is precisely the work a national name of this kind ought to do; the real-world parallel is exact, since the name of Iran descends from a word meaning noble in the same way. A placeholder that happened to land on the right idea is not a placeholder; it is a first draft that came out right.

What it wants is only a formal shape, since a bare adjective does not look like a country.

Two things come free with it. The nobility of Berdakan, over the mountains, are the azatk', the free — the same word, and a genuine cousin across the family; both parties know it and neither is entirely pleased about it. And there is an irony sitting ready in the middle of the setting’s most sophisticated civilisation: a people who call themselves the free are ruled by a Padishah who styles himself King of Kings, and the word for noble and the word for free are the same word. In Azade, freedom is a rank.

Note what the names are doing. Zarestan was Engonia and is not any longer. Asana is still called Asania in the classical tongue and answers to it. A region that has changed its name from a classical one to a native one has had a nativist dynasty pass over it; a region that has kept the old name has not. The map records a revolution in two parentheses.

III. The two houses

The archive says Azade is a kingdom and, in the next line, that the Padishah is of Zarestan. Both are true, and the second explains why the first keeps changing hands.

Zarestan

The present power, and the newer. Its claim rests upon the faith: the scripture was codified under its patronage, the great fires are in its keeping, and its Padishah is crowned by the chief priest and not by the great houses. It is agrarian, bureaucratic, literate, and governs through salaried officials who can be dismissed. Its instinct in all things is to write the matter down and keep the copy.

It is also the nativist party. It renamed Engonia, it has been purging the classical vocabulary out of the chancery for two centuries, and it holds — sincerely — that it restored something rather than founded it.

Asania

The elder, and at present the lesser. Its strength is the horse country of the south-eastern plains and the dozen or so great landed houses that breed and arm the heavy cavalry, each of which could put a few thousand armoured riders in the field without asking anybody. Its head is Bōrzmihr, who keeps his court at Augara and is scrupulously correct in his addresses to the Padishah.

It is cosmopolitan where Zarestan is nativist: it kept its classical name, its noble houses intermarry with Berdakan and with the merchant families of Nabandar, and it has never seen why a priest should crown a king.

The alternation, and what it does to history

The crown has passed between the two more than once, and each time the chronicles are rewritten. This is the single most useful thing about Azade and ought to be preserved in everything written about it hereafter.

The national epic has a hole in it. The Pādnāma, the Book of Kings, runs from the making of the world to the present dynasty in some fifty thousand couplets — and the four centuries in which Asania held the crown occupy a few hundred lines, a list of names, and no deeds worth recording. The Asanians keep their own recension in which those centuries are the glory of the world and the Zarestani restoration is an interruption. Neither house will print the other’s. Scholars in Berdakan and in Trisindhu, who have both, find the comparison instructive and know better than to say so in Zarestan.

IV. Kafestan

In the lists it is an Azadean region. In fact it is a third power, and the one a party of adventurers is most likely to meet.

It is mountain country south-west of Asana, holding the passes into Trisindhu, and it is ruled from Ghormbad by a Marzbān — a march-warden — whose office was once an Azadean appointment and is now hereditary in all but name. The present holder is Zarmihr.

The arrangement suits everybody. Kafestan acknowledges whichever house holds the crown, sends no taxes worth the name, and remits instead a share of what it takes in the south; the Padishah accepts this because the mountains are not worth the cost of governing properly, and because a march-warden who is raiding Trisindhu is not raiding Zarestan. Kafestan has gone down into the Vedan plains a dozen times in three centuries, usually through the passes above Khadam, the western mountain town of Toyāvarta, which has been taken and retaken so often that its name, its garrison and half its vocabulary are Azadean.

This is why the north-western Vedan kingdom speaks as it does. The Azadean road explains the trade; Kafestan explains the walls.

V. The Ardavan faith

The name is not a dynasty’s. Ard means Truth, and the Ardavans are the people of the Truth.

The world is divided into four elemental essences — Atar the fire, Aban the waters, Zam the earth, Vad the winds — worked upon by two parallel forces: Akvan, darkness, which manifests in the world as Doruj, deception; and Vahman, light, which manifests as Ard, truth. The whole of Creation derives from Hordad, the entirety.

Three books carry it.

  • The Hymns. Short, metrically archaic, attributed to the founder himself, in a tongue nobody has spoken for two thousand years. They are far stranger than anything built upon them, and they condemn by name a class of priest that still exists. See §VI.
  • The Zend, the commentary, in the living tongue. It is longer than the scripture by a factor of fifty and it is what is actually taught, actually argued over, and actually meant when a man says what the faith holds. The gloss has eaten the text, and the priesthood reads a commentary on a language it construes rather than understands.
  • The Hordādnāma, the Book of the Whole: the making of the world in stages, the assault of Akvan upon it, the long mixture in which we now live, and the restoration at the end of days. It is the most read and the least canonical of the three.

Their priests are sorcerers, and openly: elemental magic and astrology above all, taught in schools, examined, licensed and recorded. Mysticism is not heresy in Azade. This single fact is the reason Azade matters to the rest of the setting, and the reason every scholar in Vora who wants to study anything seriously eventually has to decide whether to go there.

VI. The old practice, and the karpān

Before any of this, the ancestors of the whole northern language family — Azade, Trisindhu, Berdakan and Vora alike — spoke with the spirits of places and worked upon the elements directly. There were no books. There were practitioners, and the practitioners were everywhere.

Azade absorbed that practice. Vora destroyed it.

The absorption is visible in the doctrine if one looks. The four essences are the four old elemental powers with a theology built over them; the priest’s licensed sorcery is the old spirit-work put into a school and given an examination; and the village practitioner was never abolished at all. He is called a karpān, a mutterer, which is the name the founder’s own Hymns use when condemning him. Every village has one. Most of the great houses keep one. The priesthood of Zarestan has been issuing edicts against them for eleven hundred years with the consistency of an institution that does not expect to be obeyed and is not sure it wishes to be.

In the north the same word came west with the missionaries as a term of abuse, and settled into Thetch as carpen: a witch, and to carpen, to mutter charms over a sick beast. The Ecclesia burns carpens. It has never remarked that the word is a loan, or asked where from.

The point of all this. The Church’s war upon mysticism is a war upon its own great-grandparents. When an Ecclesiastical court condemns a Hadur shaman for communing with spirits, it is condemning a practice its own ancestors had, which its own eastern cousins still licence, and which is named in its own scripture’s parent text. Nobody in Vora knows this. The Ardavan priesthood knows it perfectly well and considers the north to have made itself stupid on purpose.

VII. The Church’s birthplace

The archive states plainly that the philosophy and beliefs of the Ecclesia originated in the lands now ruled by Azade, and elsewhere that the Church was formed in Taldar, out of ancient human myths combined with elven philosophy. Both can stand, and the reconciliation is geographical: Bagad borders the Eborian wildlands, and the Taldar country lies beyond them. The myths are Azadean and the philosophy is elven, and the compound was made on that border and carried west.

So the Ecclesia and the Ardavan faith are not rivals from different worlds. They are siblings, which is why the archive notes clear similarities between them, and why the differences are the bitter kind rather than the puzzled kind. One sibling went west, took on an elven metaphysics, and anathematised the family’s old habits. The other stayed home and kept them.

VIII. Letters

Azade is the literary centre of the human world, and two of its four best-loved books are imports it has stopped noticing are imports.

The PādnāmaThe Book of Kings. The national epic, in couplets, from the making of the world to the present day — with the hole in it described above.
The HordādnāmaCreation, the assault, the mixture, the restoration. §V.
Vahrāz and NarmgulThe great romance: a Zarestani prince and an Asanian lady, and a bad end. Both houses read it as proving their own case, which is a fair measure of its quality.
The Book of the Two HeronsBeast-fables in a frame, two courtier-birds advising a king: a mirror for princes disguised as a bestiary. Translated out of Trisindhu, where the original is older and longer.
The Hazār ŠabThe Thousand Nights. A woman postponing an execution by never finishing a story. The frame came north with the fables; a good deal of the matter is Vedan.

The last two matter beyond Azade. They have gone on west, in Azadean versions, and are read in Vora without the faintest notion of where they came from. The most popular secular literature in the Ecumene is a third-hand translation of the scripture-adjacent storytelling of a people the Ecumene regards as barely worth a page.

IX. Azade abroad

  • Vora — the main trade route east, and the place a northern scholar goes when the Ecclesia will not let him study. Relations are correct and cold.
  • Kastaur and the Keradon frontier — the gate. Whoever holds it taxes Azade’s whole western commerce, which Azade resents and has never been willing to fight for.
  • Berdakan — the mountain neighbour, partitioned twice between Azade and the north, still there. Cousins by language, rivals by faith, and useful to both sides.
  • Trisindhu — the great partner and the standing victim: the source of the fables, much of the frame-tale matter, and a good deal of the trade, raided every generation by a march-warden the Padishah declines to restrain.
  • The Children of the Wilds — Azade does not hold them to be a category. It trades with trolls, employs Hadur as couriers, and regards the northern two-power doctrine as a provincialism. Azade is where the Seren might be undone, if anywhere, and both factions know it.

X. For play

The Azadean campaign is not a war. It is a court, a library and a road.

  • The licence. Any character who wants to learn magic lawfully must come here, be examined, and be entered in a register that the Ecclesia would very much like to read.
  • The succession. Vidarne will not live for ever, and Bōrzmihr is scrupulously correct in every address he makes, which is itself a kind of statement.
  • The recension. Somebody has a complete Asanian Pādnāma. Somebody else wants it burnt.
  • The raid. Zarmihr is going down into Toyāvarta again, and the party is either with him, in front of him, or hired by Camala to find out when.
  • The Hymns. The founder’s own words condemn the karpān, and nobody has construed them properly in two thousand years, and there is a scholar in Bagad who thinks the condemnation has been read backwards.

What is still wanting

  • No dates. The Zarestani restoration, the length of the Asanian centuries and the founder’s own era are all unfixed, and the Pādnāma's hole needs a number to be a hole.
  • No capital named for Zarestan. The archive gives towns for every other region and none for the central one.
  • The founder has no name. Everything in §V hangs off him.
  • The old practice has no name of its own in the ancestral tongue, only the Azadean slur for its practitioners. Trisindhu will have kept a politer word, and should supply it.
  • Whether Jangestan’s war is still live, and against what. The archive makes it a buffer against the Eborian Wilderness; it would do double duty as the north-eastern march if the new map wants one.
  • Asania’s great houses are a dozen in principle and none on paper.
