The Vedan Kingdoms

Trisindhu · त्रिसिन्धु · the Three Rivers · the southernmost kingdoms of Man

Heading south-east from Azade and crossing the mountainous highlands of Kafestan and southern Asana, one comes at length to be surrounded by dense forest. These jungles mark the beginning of a great region, thriving with life.

The Vedan Kingdoms are the far south-east of the known world, and the only part of it in which the Children of the Heavens and the Children of the Wilds are not understood to be at war. They are rich, populous, old, hot, and almost wholly unknown to the Voraic west, which has had the whole account of them from Azadean intermediaries and has not thought to check it.

Vedan is a western word, out of the name of the classical empire of Vaidana, which fell about the time Sarcera did. The kingdoms call their own country Trisindhu, the Three Rivers, after the Ujjvalā, the Timirā and the Gabhīrā.

I. The land

The region runs from the Kafestan highlands in the north-west down and east into jungle. It is not one country but four, with a desert belt dividing the north-western kingdom from the three forest kingdoms, and a long coast at either end of it.

The three rivers. The Ujjvalā, the Shining, through the central farmlands; the Timirā, the Dark, out of the eastern hills; the Gabhīrā, the Deep, southward to the sea through a delta of some hundreds of miles of marsh.

The Kara marks the western limit, and beyond it is Gantumba.

II. The four kingdoms

Toyāvarta — तोयावर्त — the Water-Country

Mulk-e-Āb to its own chancery. The north-westernmost and the oldest Vedan foothold, lying between the mountains and the northern sea, with desert at its back. Ruled at present by Rājā Angiras, lately succeeded upon the death of Gadan II, from Camala, a city set centrally upon the shore of Lake Toyacan.

Toyāvarta is the kingdom the user’s brief calls Urdu-leaning, and the reason is geography. Its kings are Vedan and bear Vedan names; its chancery, its revenue rolls, its poetry and the speech of its towns have all been Persianised by four hundred years of the Azadean road. A clerk in Camala writes his master’s Sanskrit name at the head of a document composed in a tongue half Persian. Nobody there finds this remarkable; everybody in Suvarṇagaṛh does.

It is also where the Ardavan faith has real purchase, and where the Arka doctrine — the Church of the Eternal Sun — keeps its only mission in the Vedan world. The mission has been there eighty years and has made perhaps two hundred converts.

Kanakadeśa — कनकदेश — the Golden Country

The core, the largest, the richest, and the one that considers itself the inheritor of Vaidana. It lies along the Ujjvalā in the central plain. Capital Suvarṇagaṛh. Its court speech is the Sanskrit-leaning register — the śuddh bhāṣā, the pure speech — and its pandits regard the northern dialect as a corruption and the eastern as a rustication.

Towns: Ratnakūṭa, the jewel-market; Padmasara, upon its lake; Brahmaghāṭ, the pilgrimage stair, where the Ujjvalā turns.

Timirāvana — तिमिरावन — the Dark-River Forest

Eastward, older, poorer and by a long way the strangest. Its chronicles are in Prakrit, not Sanskrit, and go back further than Kanakadeśa’s. It is forest nearly throughout, it keeps elephants, and it is the only human kingdom anywhere on civil terms with the Kar’aqa. Capital Niśāntapura, the Town at the End of Night.

Towns: Gajamārga, the elephant-road; Chāyādvāra, the shadow-gate; Ukkalakoṭa, which was an Ukkal town and is now a Vedan one, and whose people will tell you so.

Samudrānta — समुद्रान्त — the Sea’s End

The southern and maritime kingdom, on the lower Gabhīrā and the delta coast. Capital Mīnadvāra, the Fish-Gate. It is the Vedan world’s door: it trades south to Gantumba and the islands, west to the Kothari coast, and occasionally with ships out of the Westerlands whose crews it regards as a curiosity. It has the largest foreign quarters, the loosest religion and the worst reputation.

Towns: Śaṅkhapura, the conch-port; Lavaṇakheṭa, the salt-flats; Naukāghāṭ, the boatyards.

III. What came before

Vaidana, the empire, held all four of these countries and some that are now jungle. Its capital was Śatadvāra, the Hundred Gates, a ruin on the upper Ujjvalā in which nobody lives and to which everybody goes. It fell roughly when Sarcera fell, and the Vedan kingdoms are successor states in exactly the sense that Ascania and Darde are — which is a parallel no Voraic scholar has noticed, the Vedan Kingdoms being filed in the west under curiosities rather than under history.

The Ukkal were there before any of it. They are the region’s substrate people: not Indo-European, not related to the Vedan tongues or to anything else now spoken, and almost wholly absorbed. The evidence that they existed at all is linguistic and overwhelming.

  • The river-names Timirā and Gabhīrā are not Sanskrit. They are Ukkal timra and gabir, re-cut by folk etymology into words meaning dark and deep, which they do not mean.
  • Toyacan does not parse as Sanskrit either; the first element has been pulled toward toya, water, which is what the lake is.
  • The southern Vedan vernaculars carry a set of words with no Indo-European cousins — for rice, fish, boat, crocodile, net and the monsoon — and a retroflex series heavier than the northern dialects can account for.

Hold-outs remain in two places: the Ukkal Hills between Timirāvana and the sea, where some dozens of villages still speak Ukkali; and the deep marshes of the Gabhīrā delta, where the boat-people are reckoned Ukkal by everybody except themselves.

IV. The Battle of Aśmaghāṭ

About twelve hundred years ago the trolls of Gantumba crossed the Kara in the First Great Conquest, took the Kothari lands, hunted the goatmen there for sport, and turned east expecting more of the same. They met instead a well-organised kingdom with a standing army, and were broken at the Stone Ford and driven back over the Kara.

This is the central fact of Vedan self-regard, and it ought to be understood before anything else about the region. The Vedan Kingdoms are the only human power that has beaten a Great Conquest in the field. The west was not there, did not help, and in most of its chronicles does not mention it. A century later the Second Great Conquest went north-west instead, and the Vedans have drawn their own conclusions about why.

It is also why the Vedan kingdoms are not nervous, where the northern kingdoms are. They have met the worst thing the Children of the Wilds ever sent against men, and won.

The Do’ari have four names, and which one a man uses tells you where he is from.

  • यक्ष yakṣa in Kanakadeśa: treasure-keepers, a term of respect, used of a wealthy and settled people who live in the cities and lend money.
  • गुह्यक guhyaka, the hidden ones, in Toyāvarta: secretive, from a northern kingdom that sees few of them and distrusts what it sees.
  • अच्छरा achcharā on the coast: the vernacular of apsarā, a dancing-girl of heaven. Admiring and not quite respectable.
  • वानर vānara, monkey, in Timirāvana — the rudest of the four, and the only one coined by people who actually live among them, who have noticed the tails.

The Hadur may not exist. Piśāca is the word, and the belief is that they were made by रुधिर Rudhira, the god of blood and of war, for his own use. But Trisindhu lies a very long way from the Hadur, and hardly anybody in it has seen one. The learned accordingly divide: some hold the piśāca a distinct creation; others that piśāca is merely another name for rākhas, there being quite enough of those to be going on with; and a third party, more travelled, reports that in far countries there are hairy rākhas with horns, which settles the matter to its own satisfaction and nobody else’s.

The Vedans are, as it happens, closer to the truth than the Ecclesia is. They are wrong about who made the Hadur and right that it was done deliberately.

The dwarves are a rumour. No Hyn’ral has ever had any presence in Trisindhu. What is known of them comes from Azadean books, and what those books describe — an underground people, long-lived, immensely rich, keepers of what is in the earth — maps so exactly onto the yakṣa of Vedan tradition that a respectable body of opinion holds the khanipuruṣa of the north to be simply yakṣa under another sky. This is the same error as the piśāca question, made by the same method, and nobody has noticed that either.

Gnomes are kūṣmāṇḍa, a class of minor goblin-spirit, which is what the region has decided to file them under. Giants are daitya. A thing out of the Astral is asura; the Demon Lords are asurendra; Marach is Mārak. A dragon is ajdahā in the street and nāga in a book.

VI. Where it stands in the quarrel

Nowhere, and it is pleased about that.

The two-power division is a north-western doctrine and does not describe Trisindhu at all. The Vedan kingdoms have no dwarves to be allied with, trade cordially with a Kar’aqa country, hold the trolls to be an old enemy beaten long ago rather than a cosmic principle, and are not at all sure the goatmen are real. When an Arka missionary explains the Children of the Heavens and the Children of the Wilds to a pandit of Suvarṇagaṛh, the pandit’s objection is not theological. It is that the scheme has six peoples in it and he can only account for three.

The contrast with the north is the point of the region. In Vora, knowing a Kar’aqa is a scandal. In Niśāntapura, the Kar’aqa of the Crow Hills — Ṭe’k’ah is their own name for the place, which the Vedans write Ṭekāh — compute the calendar by which the kingdom sows its rice, in exchange for salt, paper and safe passage. The arrangement is three hundred years old. No Voraic churchman has been told about it, and the Timirāvanese see no reason to begin.

What is still wanting

  • No dates for Vaidana beyond "about when Sarcera fell", and no king-list.
  • Rudhira wants a pantheon around him. The Vedan religion is at present one god and an implication.
  • The Ukkal need a word-list — twenty words would settle the substrate properly, and the engine could draw them to a phonotactics as it does for Orthrus.
  • Carcatha, Khadam and Marthan are names only, as the archive left them.
  • The Kar’aqa country has one place-name and no people. It is the only Kar’aqa culture anywhere in the setting that is on speaking terms with men, and is therefore the most useful one to develop.
  • Angiras’s dynasty, and what Gadan II died of, are open and probably ought to be a hook.
