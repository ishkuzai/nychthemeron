The Shape of the Old World

Endaran · settled 14 October 2026

The continent runs east from a small, crowded, self-important north-west corner into country that gets larger, older and stranger the further one goes. Vora is not the middle of it. Vora is the end of it, and the only reason the maps are drawn with Vora in the centre is that the maps are drawn in Vora.

I. Two axes

There are two ways east, and they share only their western end.

RunsCharacter
The continental axisVora → the Urthan Mountains → the Eastern Kingdoms → Tucania → the far-eastern range → the elven eastCold, landlocked, scarcely peopled, crossed by one road. Severia alone has a coast.
The maritime axisVora → the Keradon frontier → the Daracian Sea → Azade → the Kafestan and Asana highlands → TrisindhuWarm, watered, ancient, thickly peopled. Every stage of it is a port or a pass.

Tucania is not on the Daracian and never was; it lies far to the north-east, beyond the Urthan range, at the far end of the Eastern Kingdoms. The Daracian belongs to the southern journey alone, and is wrapped by Urama to the north, Azade to the west and Trisindhu to the south. It is a sea of the Black Sea sort: a real sea with a real coast and real shipping, not an enclosed salt pan.

The consequence is that there is no thin arm anywhere. The land between the northern world and the southern one is the whole breadth of the Eastern Kingdoms, the Urthan range and Azade besides.

II. The southern frontier

This is the most important structure on the map and the one most often got wrong. It is not a wall from sea to sea. It is a frontier system of four parts, running west to east, which divides Vora from everything south and east of it.

1. The western ground, and Evander’s Wall

Between the western ocean and the foot of the mountains lies an open stretch of some seventy or eighty miles — the only level approach into Vora from the south. Across it the Sarcerans built the wall: Evander’s Wall, or the Wall of Naldor, raised under Caeso Evander after his predecessor Carterus had spent two decades and a good share of the treasury failing to conquer Eboria by arms. A Haldoracian architect, Naldor, was sent by the King of Haldorak with ten thousand dwarven workers and a regiment of a thousand warriors from each Haldan fort-city to guard the building.

It is a wall of that length and no more. Sea to sea is how the chronicles put it, and the chronicles are describing the whole frontier from the western ocean to the Daracian — wall, mountains, gate and all — as though it were one work of masonry. It was never that. The phrase is Sarceran rhetoric that has hardened into received fact, which is the ordinary career of Sarceran rhetoric.

2. The Keradon Mountains

East of the wall the range rises and does the rest of the work for nothing. The Keradon runs from the wall’s eastern terminus to the shore of the Daracian, and is impassable to an army along its whole length but for one place.

3. Naldor’s Gate

At the one pass stands the fortification, built by the same dwarven hands and in the same years as the wall, and named for the same architect. It has never been taken. In fourteen hundred years it has been besieged, bought, betrayed and twice abandoned, and it has not once been carried by assault.

It is also falling down. The works are dwarven and the method of their making went into the mountain with Haldorak two thousand years ago. What repairs have been attempted since are patches by men upon a structure they do not understand, and every garrison commander for the last four centuries has reported the same thing in the same words to a Council that cannot act upon it. The one fortification nobody can take is the one nobody can mend.

4. Kastaur

At the eastern end the Keradon dips steeply to the Daracian, and in that gap stands Kastaur, anciently Castorum, a distant province of the Sarceran Empire. It began as a fort-town closing the coastal road and became something far more useful: the place where the overland route from Vora to the southern human kingdoms meets the sea route to everywhere else. Whoever holds Kastaur holds the junction of the two, and takes a portion of everything that passes either way.

The Urams conquered the region some five hundred years ago, and the Kingdom of Urama now sits upon the northern shore of the Daracian with Kastaur for its capital.

Open. Whether the Urams took the city or only the country about it is the one decision outstanding here. Taking the country alone leaves a Sarceran remnant holding Kastaur and a strip of coast — five centuries of unfinished siege, the old titles and the old calendar still in use, and a toll upon every caravan going east. It costs one word in the archive and gives the Council of Kings an embarrassment it can neither acknowledge nor abandon.

III. What this does to Vora

It makes Vora small, and that is the point.

The traditional-fantasy country — kings, cathedrals, dwarven halls, forests with things in them — is a north-western corner of a continent that extends very much further east than it is comfortable about. It is large enough to hold a campaign without ever leaving it, and small enough that the Church’s claim to speak for the world is quietly absurd. The Ecumene is a peninsula that believes itself an ecumene.

This also gives the settled proportion in the archive its proper force: the Eastern Kingdoms alone are easily as big as the region of Vora, if not even bigger — and the Eastern Kingdoms are one region of several east of the Urthan. Vora should be held to something nearer France, Germany, the Low Countries and Iberia together than to all Europe.

IV. The Seren

The desert is not a climate. It was savannah eight thousand years ago; the magi of Sotla made it what it is in an attempt to save their own kingdom; and it is still expanding. It therefore owes nothing to latitude and may run on whatever line suits the map — on the diagonal, widening from its centre, rather than in a horizontal band.

This is also the second half of why the frontier is shaped as it is. West of the Keradon the ground is open and had to be walled. East and south of the Keradon the Seren does the walling, and grows a little better at it every year.

V. Two minor kingdoms

Proposals, 14 October. The compendium’s open question 10 — "Armenian and Tocharian have no people" — with the placements amended to suit the settled map.

Both are deliberately small: a province’s worth of detail, not a nation’s. They exist to vary the texture of the northern language family without adding another great power.

Berdakan — the kingdom in the mountains

Stand-in tongue: Armenian. Berdakan, the land of fortresses; capital Shoghaberd; their holy mountain Hadzor; their nobility the azatk', the free.

A highland kingdom in the Keradon, between Azade and Vora, holding ground that both have wanted and neither has been able to keep. It has been partitioned between them twice and has survived both partitions by outlasting the partitioners.

Three things make it worth having on the map.

  • It took the Light of the Heavens before Sarcera did, by a generation or two, and has never once let anybody forget it. Its church is in communion with the Ecclesia on terms that satisfy neither party, and its patriarch signs himself with a style the Ecclesia declines to print.
  • It has its own alphabet, invented outright by a churchman — Nerseh the Scribe — rather than inherited from the Anachoran as everyone else’s was. It is the only script in the northern world that owes nothing to the Abecedarium Imperii.
  • It garrisons Naldor’s Gate, and has for about a thousand years, and the Council of Kings pays it to go on doing so because no Voraic kingdom wishes to station troops that far from home. A small mountain people are paid by their betters to hold the door, and have noticed.

Azatk' and Azade are the same word, which the Berdakani know and the Azadeans enjoy pointing out.

Wertsyan — the assembly of the oases

Stand-in tongue: Tocharian. A confederation rather than a kingdom: the oasis towns of Śanke, Ārśil and Pälkone, beyond the far-eastern mountains, at the margin of the elven lands. Wertsyan is their own word for the assembly of the towns, and serves for the people, the tongue and the polity together.

They speak the easternmost tongue of the northern family, and an archaic one: it has kept distinctions that Thetch and the Imperial tongue lost before either was written down, which is what happens to a language carried a very long way and then left alone. Scarcely anyone in the west knows they exist.

Two hooks sit ready in the existing material.

  • Their written literature is almost entirely translation, and nearly all of it is scripture out of Trisindhu, carried north-east over the mountains by missionaries some centuries ago. A people of the northern language family hold a southern faith in a tongue nobody else can read. The Ecclesia has never heard of any of it.
  • Tucania is named after them. They held those plains before the Tulkata came down into them and retreated east into the oases; the name stayed behind. So the Great Settlement is the survey of a region claimed by the Tulkata, named for the Wertsyan, and chartered by the Great Empire in the East — three claims on one country, which is how frontiers generally work.

A note on the archive’s earlier proposal. The compendium suggested Tsevrokh and Herran Thagal for Armenian, and Tucania and the Yat for Tocharian. Berdakan supersedes the first placement, Armenian belonging between two empires rather than in the far east. Tsevrokh and Herran Thagal are better used as the non-Indo-European highland pair the Lore Overview already describes — the oldest cultures in their region, impervious to outside influence, and bearing a superficial resemblance to one another that nothing else explains. That resemblance is the whole point of them: two survivals of something that was there first.

What is still wanting

  • The Keradon Mountains want their Sarceran name and their pre-Sarceran one; the range is older than the people who named it on the maps.
  • Whether Urama holds Kastaur itself (§II.4).
  • The length and date of Evander’s Wall are now fixed in principle and not in figures.
  • The Berdakani alphabet would repay an actual letter-chart, as the Derathi script has.
  • The Wertsyan tongue has three town-names and nothing else. If it is to be the archaic member of the northern family, a dozen words showing the retained distinctions would settle it, and the engine can draw them.
  • Hattar, the Kothari and Eboria are south of the frontier and have no page.
